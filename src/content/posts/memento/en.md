Christopher Nolan’s first films are counted among his early works. Speaking from personal experience, at first I didn’t understand the beginning of the movie at all. It was only after about 20 minutes that I grasped how the film was progressing and what it was about.

Imagine…

Your short‑term memory works for only 10‑15 minutes. After that you can’t recall what happened, where you are, or who the person in front of you is. Our main character Leonard (played by Guy Pearce) lives in exactly such a terrible state. After his wife’s tragic death he suffers a severe head injury. Now the only and biggest purpose in his life is to find the killer who murdered his wife.

But how? After all, he can’t even remember who he just talked to, right? For that he devises a uniquely strange, yet forced, system. He always carries a Polaroid camera, takes pictures of everything, and writes notes on the back. The most important facts, names and addresses he must never forget, he gets tattooed on his own body. Think about it—your own body becomes your only reliable diary.

![](https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcQro83kgrdqKc5jZ54FcFPZkIQg9hEwlftOd1R7CBZeKdBX2pUij0r4je4X&s=10)

The brain‑exhausting (but brilliant) structure

What made this film so popular among everyone is the way the events are presented. Nolan chose a ruthless yet genius method to put us into Leonard’s condition. The film’s events do not run from beginning to end in the usual way, but from the end to the beginning!

Each time a new scene starts, just like the hero, we have no idea where we are, whether the people around us are friends or enemies. Only when we reach the end of a scene (i.e., when we see the chronologically earlier event) do we conclude, “Ah, so that’s why he ended up here.” In short, the film is divided into black‑and‑white (straight chronology) and colored (reverse chronology) scenes, which merge at a single point at the end, solving all the puzzles at once.

How do we deceive ourselves?

In fact, “Memento” is not just a simple story about revenge. It’s about how unreliable human memory is. Think about it: we know who we actually are through our memories, right? What if our memory deceives us?

Throughout the film the question arises whether Leonard’s papers, photos, and notes are truly trustworthy. The scariest part is whether he is creating his own “truth.” The film forces the viewer to contemplate a heavy truth: sometimes a person may prefer to comfort themselves with a lie rather than know the truth, in order to give their life meaning and avoid aimlessness.

In short…

“Memento” demands your full, hundred‑percent attention. If you get distracted by your phone to send a message, you can completely lose the thread. But if you enjoy using your brain, searching for meaning in every detail, and thinking about the film for hours after it ends — this movie is exactly for you. The first time you watch it you’ll be busy piecing together all the parts; the second time you’ll discover entirely new, deeper meanings.
